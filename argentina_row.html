Argentina 🇦🇷
Last updated: 2026.11.26
Very few restrictionsPrior censorship is constitutionally prohibited (Constitution Arts. 14 & 32, Art. 13 American Convention on Human Rights). Law 26.032 (2005) extends freedom of expression to the Internet, and Law 26.551 (2009) decriminalised slander and libel on matters of public interest. Liability only arises afterwards for offences such as threats, incitement to crime, or non-consensual intimate imagery (Ley Olimpia, Law 27.736). In 2026 the Supreme Court ruled that anonymous speech is protected but not absolutely: anonymity can be lifted in cases of defamation or invasion of privacy.
Yes, court-ordered blocksThere is no central government filter and no administrative blocking for political reasons, but federal and commercial judges routinely order ISPs to block domains at DNS or IP level. Targets are almost exclusively sports-streaming piracy (e.g. Fútbol Libre, Roja Directa; over 180 domains blocked at once in 11/2026), Cuevana, Magis TV, and unlicensed gambling sites reported by provincial regulators (Law 11.723, intellectual property). See ENACOM's list of court-ordered blocks.
No strict liability, but judicial pressureArgentina has no specific intermediary-liability law. In Rodríguez, María Belén v. Google (2014) the Supreme Court held that platforms and search engines are not liable for third-party content unless they have actual knowledge of manifestly illegal content and fail to act. For content that is not manifestly illegal (e.g. defamation), a court order is required before removal or de-indexing. See the ruling and the later Denegri v. Google case.
No bansNo law prohibits or restricts strong or end-to-end encryption, and no law requires backdoors or client-side scanning. The confidentiality of private communications is constitutionally inviolable (Constitution Art. 18) and may only be intercepted under a reasoned court order (Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 236).
No bansNo law bans or restricts VPNs, Tor or I2P. Law 27.078 (Argentina Digital) establishes the principle of net neutrality in the telecom framework.
No, but proposedNo law currently requires age or ID verification for social media or adult websites beyond platforms' own self-declarations. In 11/2026, Bill 6018-D-2026 was introduced in the Chamber of Deputies. It would require biometric identity and age verification via Renaper ("proof of life") to open or keep accounts on social media, e-commerce and online gaming platforms (purely passive/informational accounts excluded). Digital rights groups warn of surveillance and data-leak risks. See Clarín's coverage.
NoNo law or technical regulation requires operating system makers or app stores to verify users' age at device level or via government APIs (see Law 25.326 on data protection and Law 26.061 on children's rights, neither of which does so).
Passwords no, biometrics yes (court rulings, no specific law)The right against self-incrimination (Constitution Art. 18) means most case law holds a defendant cannot be compelled to reveal passwords, PINs or patterns (e.g. the 2016 Fifth Panel ruling in "C., W. J."), and refusal cannot count against them. However, courts distinguish "knowing" from "being": in the "Mora" case and a 07/2025 Buenos Aires ruling, they upheld court orders to forcibly unlock phones with fingerprint, iris or face, by analogy with CPP Art. 218 bis (DNA/fingerprint collection). Dissenting judges argue this lacks an express legal basis and violates Art. 18 and Law 25.326.
No bans, but restrictionsHolding, using and P2P trading of privacy coins such as Monero or Zcash is legal, and self-custody wallets are expressly excluded from regulated entities. However, exchanges and custodians (VASPs) must register with the CNV (General Resolution 1172/2026), run strict KYC, and report suspicious transactions to the UIF (UIF Resolution 49/2024), which in practice discourages local platforms from listing anonymous coins.
NoneLaw 25.873 (the 2004 "Spy Law") required telecoms to retain all users' traffic data for 10 years, but the Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional in Halabi, Ernesto v. P.E.N. (2009) for enabling mass surveillance without a court order. Providers now keep data only for technical or billing purposes and hand it over only on an individual court order.
Yes, must register with official ID (DNI) and biometricsAnonymous SIM cards are prohibited. Under ENACOM Resolutions 263/2023 and 1391/2024 (fully in force since 03/2025), every mobile line must be registered to a person or legal entity. Activating a line or replacing a SIM requires the DNI number, gender, and a biometric proof-of-life check against Renaper's database.
Limited, app required for key features (Android/iOS)AUTENTICAR, the federated login platform (OpenID Connect/SAML), lets users sign in from any browser and OS with username and password, plus optional 2FA by OTP/email, for services such as ARCA, ANSES and the Mi Argentina website. However, the Mi Argentina app, which holds official digital credentials, electronic prescriptions and high-security procedures, is only available for Android and iOS, and initial registration requires biometric facial recognition through it.
